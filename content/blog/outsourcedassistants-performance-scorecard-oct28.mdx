## Why this control matters

Performance Scorecard Without Vanity Metrics is a record, not a reminder to be careful. It shows what the team checked, who owned the next step, and what remained open before the client acted. The control matters because the team celebrates volume while rework and open items grow.

## Open the governing record

Start with the scorecard and sample. Record the source, the version or date observed, the owner, and the field the evidence supports. Save the original rather than retyping it into a summary. The assistant prepares and checks the record; the account owner keeps the decision that changes a client commitment, a payment, a schedule, or access.

## Where the handoff breaks

The predictable failure is this: the team celebrates volume while rework and open items grow. It becomes expensive when the next owner cannot tell whether the item is a request, an approval, or a correction. Use one status set: ready, exception, approved, and closed. A status without an owner and a date is only a label.

## A worked outsourced example

An assistant completes many tasks but the rework rate rises. The scorecard shows both numbers beside the open-item age. The point is not the specific number. It is that the record shows the original state, the change, the reviewer, and the confirmation in one place. Review the example with the person who owns assistant performance scorecard. If they cannot explain the close condition from the record alone, the control is not finished.

## Weekly review and stop rule

Sample one ordinary item, one exception, and one recently corrected item. Track completeness and the age of what remains open. Count errors by rule rather than by person. When the same gap appears twice, fix the form, the routing rule, or the source map before coaching the same operator again.
