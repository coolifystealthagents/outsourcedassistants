# Set expiry rules for executive calendar holds

A tentative hold solves a real scheduling problem: it protects a possible meeting time while someone confirms the details. The trouble starts when nobody defines how the hold ends. An old hold can block a customer call, hide genuine availability, or force an executive to make a rushed choice. A useful calendar routine treats every hold as a temporary reservation with a reason, an owner, and a deadline.

## Decide what the hold is protecting

Start with the decision that remains open. A hold might protect time while an external attendee confirms, while a meeting owner chooses between two slots, or while travel details settle. Those are different cases. Record the meeting purpose, requester, people affected, time zone, duration, decision owner, and latest useful confirmation time. Do not use a vague title such as "Hold" when an approved private label can identify the request without exposing sensitive context.

The assistant also needs to know whether the hold is exclusive. Some teams permit several clearly labeled options for one meeting. Others treat the first hold as unavailable time. Write that rule down. Otherwise two assistants can honestly read the same calendar and reach opposite conclusions about whether a slot may be offered.

## Give each hold a state

A small set of states makes the queue reviewable. "Awaiting attendee" means the proposed participant has not replied. "Awaiting owner" means the executive or meeting owner must choose. "Ready to confirm" means all required facts are present but the invitation has not been sent. "Expired" means the stated decision time passed without the required approval. The labels should describe evidence, not optimism.

Add the state to a calendar-safe field or the approved scheduling tracker. Include the source request and the next review time. Avoid copying private email, commercial terms, health information, or relationship context into a broadly visible event. The calendar needs enough information to manage the reservation, not a transcript of the conversation.

## Set expiry from the scheduling consequence

The deadline should reflect what happens if the slot remains blocked. A hold for tomorrow morning may need a same-day answer. A possible quarterly review several weeks away may tolerate a longer window. Work backward from the time needed to notify attendees, prepare materials, protect travel or focus buffers, and offer the released time to someone else.

Use an exact time and named zone. "End of day" is ambiguous when an executive, assistant, and attendee work in different countries. A record such as "expires 3:00 p.m. America/New_York on October 2" gives everyone one instant to act on. Calendar software may display local time differently, so include the named zone in the supporting record.

An expiry is not permission to decline an important request. It tells the assistant what to do with the reservation. The approved action might be to release it, shorten it, replace it with a new option, or escalate it to the owner. If the consequences involve a customer commitment, a board matter, travel, a confidential event, or a protected personal appointment, keep the decision with the authorized owner.

## Work through a realistic collision

Suppose an investor asks for two possible times next week. The executive approves tentative holds on Tuesday and Thursday while another attendee checks availability. On Wednesday morning, a customer asks for the Thursday slot. The Thursday hold expires at noon Wednesday, but the Tuesday option remains workable.

The assistant should not silently delete Thursday or tell the customer it is available. First, check the recorded expiry, confirmation status, and authority rule. Then send the approved reminder to the decision owner with the relevant facts: which option remains, who is waiting, when the customer needs an answer, and what will happen at noon. If no answer arrives and the standing rule permits release, record the expiry and release Thursday. Preserve Tuesday until its own deadline. The evidence should show why each slot had a different outcome.

Now change one fact. If Thursday is the only time the investor can attend, releasing it may amount to choosing the customer meeting over the investor meeting. That is a priority decision, not calendar housekeeping. The assistant should present the conflict and wait for the executive or chief of staff.

## Review renewals instead of extending by habit

A renewal needs a new reason and deadline. Repeated extensions often mean the request lacks an owner, the options are premature, or the meeting is not ready to schedule. Track how many times a hold has moved or renewed. After the allowed limit, return the request for a decision instead of creating another layer of unavailable time.

Review the oldest holds daily. Check whether the source request still exists, the attendees remain correct, the time zone has changed, a related event was confirmed, or the decision owner is away. Release duplicates and superseded options under the written rule. Escalate holds whose business importance or privacy cannot be judged from the scheduling record.

## Measure whether the rule improves the calendar

Count holds created, confirmed before expiry, released under rule, renewed, and escalated. Also review owner reversals, attendee confusion, and cases where a stale hold displaced real work. Separate assistant handling time from time spent waiting for a decision. A low confirmation rate does not automatically show poor scheduling; it may show that requesters are reserving options too early.

Sample both ordinary and consequential holds. Compare the request, authority, timestamp, reminders, final action, and attendee communication. If reviewers disagree, improve the rule or examples before increasing the assistant's authority.
