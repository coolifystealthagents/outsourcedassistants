# Compare flight connections with a decision brief

The shortest itinerary is not always the easiest trip. A tight connection can save an hour on paper while creating a larger chance of a missed meeting, separated baggage, an unplanned terminal transfer, or an overnight delay. An assistant preparing travel options should make those tradeoffs visible. The traveler or travel manager still chooses the itinerary.

## Begin with the purpose of the trip

Connection risk depends on what happens after arrival. A traveler attending an evening dinner has a different margin than someone speaking shortly after landing. Record the first fixed commitment, its location and time zone, the ground journey from the airport, and the latest acceptable arrival. Add any approved recovery requirement, such as avoiding the final flight of the day.

Collect only the profile information needed for the comparison. Relevant facts may include mobility needs, checked baggage, an approved cabin, loyalty membership, and the travel policy. Identity documents, payment details, medical explanations, and passport copies belong in approved systems with limited access. They should not appear in a general comparison sheet.

## Compare the whole connection

Scheduled minutes between flights are only the starting point. Note whether both segments use the same ticket, whether the traveler must change terminals, collect and recheck baggage, clear immigration or security, or travel between airports. Record whether the onward flight operates frequently enough to provide a practical recovery option.

Use the airline and airport sources available for the proposed itinerary. Do not turn a general minimum connection time into a promise. Published schedules can change, gates are assigned later, and individual processing time varies. The brief should say which facts were confirmed, when they were checked, and which conditions remain unknown.

A useful comparison has one row per itinerary. Include departure and arrival, named time zones, connection airport, connection duration, terminal or airport change, baggage path, border step, final-flight status, later same-day options, change restrictions, and the decision deadline. Link to the live booking source rather than copying sensitive profile data.

## Distinguish feasibility from tolerance

An itinerary may be bookable without fitting the traveler's risk tolerance. Label that difference. "Offered by the carrier" describes availability. It does not establish that the route protects the business purpose of the trip.

Consider two options from Manila to a client meeting. Option A arrives at 2:20 p.m. after a 55-minute connection. The meeting starts at 6:00 p.m., and ground travel is expected before check-in. Option B costs more time, has a three-hour connection, and arrives at 4:10 p.m. the previous day. The assistant can show the timing, steps, fare conditions, and recovery paths. The traveler decides whether the earlier departure and overnight stay are justified.

Now suppose Option A is the last onward flight that day. A missed connection would move arrival past the meeting. That fact changes the consequence even if 55 minutes meets the booking system's rule. The brief should place it next to the meeting deadline, not bury it in a note.

## Show what happens when a segment fails

For each serious option, identify the next plausible route after a missed connection. Record whether it is later the same day, the following morning, or unavailable without changing carriers or airports. Do not claim that a seat will be available. The purpose is to compare recovery shape, not predict airline operations.

Check whether separate tickets shift baggage transfer and rebooking responsibility to the traveler. Flag self-transfers clearly. If the itinerary involves a visa, entry permission, transit rule, health requirement, or passport-validity question, route it to the traveler and an authoritative government or carrier source. An assistant should not interpret eligibility or guarantee admission.

## Use a clear recommendation boundary

The assistant may rank options under an approved rule, such as preferring one-ticket itineraries that arrive before the stated buffer. The brief must show the rule. It should not present a personal judgment as policy.

Escalate when every option breaks a stated constraint, the traveler must choose between cost and a protected commitment, the route uses separate tickets, an airport transfer is unclear, or the available evidence conflicts. Also escalate requests to alter identity data, accessibility arrangements, payment, or a booked trip with material penalties.

Set an expiry time for the comparison. Fares, schedules, and availability change. A brief checked yesterday may still explain the route, but it cannot claim today's price or seat. Before booking, recheck the chosen itinerary in the authorized source and record the final approval.

## Review the booking handoff

After approval, compare the booked record with the selected row. Check names only in the protected booking system, then verify dates, local times, airports, terminals if assigned, flight numbers, baggage information, seats requested, and ground-transfer assumptions. Send the approved itinerary through the agreed channel. Remove temporary exports according to the organization's retention rule.

Review a sample of trips after travel. Look at missed or rushed connections, baggage problems, owner overrides, schedule changes, and cases where the recovery path mattered. Separate problems caused by a later airline change from errors in the original comparison. Raw bookings per hour do not measure whether the traveler received a useful decision.

Track how often a chosen itinerary violated a stated buffer, how often critical facts were missing at approval, and how often an owner changed the assistant's ranking. Repeated overrides can reveal an undocumented preference. Add that preference to the travel brief only after the traveler or travel manager confirms it.

## Sources and limits

The International Air Transport Association publishes passenger baggage and travel information. The U.S. Department of Transportation publishes consumer guidance about air travel, including cancellations and delays for covered travel. Government immigration authorities and airlines publish route-specific entry and transit requirements. NIST SP 800-46 Rev. 2 discusses remote-access security. These sources can support careful research and data handling, but none can guarantee a connection, seat, border decision, or recovery outcome. Recheck itinerary-specific information before booking and keep the final decision with the authorized traveler or travel manager.
