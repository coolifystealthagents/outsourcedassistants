# Build a context pack for a decision meeting

A crowded pre-read can leave a meeting less prepared, not more. Participants spend their time finding the current number, arguing about which document controls, or discovering that the real decision belongs to someone who was not invited. A decision context pack should reduce that search. It gives the meeting owner a short, traceable account of the choice, the evidence, and the unresolved points.

An assistant can assemble the pack. The meeting owner remains responsible for framing the decision, approving access, and choosing an outcome.

## Write the decision before collecting documents

Begin with one sentence that names the choice and the person authorized to make it. "Review the supplier" is an activity. "Choose whether to renew the supplier for the next term under the approved budget" is a decision. The second version tells the assistant which facts matter and which material belongs elsewhere.

Record the deadline and what happens if no decision is made. A meeting that can slip a week needs different preparation from one tied to a contract notice period or customer commitment. Do not invent a consequence. Ask the owner to confirm it.

Next, list the permitted outcomes. They might include approve, reject, request more evidence, or defer until a named condition is met. Avoid a false binary when the owner has another valid path. At the same time, do not add alternatives that internal policy does not allow.

## Separate facts from proposals

Use visible labels. Confirmed facts should link to the governing record and include an observation date. Assumptions should name their author and show why they matter. Proposals should name the person or team making them. Open questions should have an owner and a latest useful answer time.

This separation prevents a polished recommendation from looking like an approved fact. It also lets a participant challenge one assumption without reopening every part of the pack.

Suppose an operations team is deciding whether to change its customer support hours. The assistant gathers recent contact volume, staffing coverage, unresolved cases, customer commitments, and the proposed schedule. A manager estimates that the new hours will reduce response delays. That estimate belongs under analysis or assumption until the evidence and method support it. The pack should not state the reduction as a promised result.

## Choose the minimum evidence set

For each fact, ask what decision question it answers. If a document does not change an option, constraint, risk, or required approval, link it as background or leave it out. A context pack is not an archive.

Prefer stable links to approved systems. State the document title, owner, version or date, and the relevant section. Do not download and duplicate restricted files simply to make the pack self-contained. Check that invited participants can open each required link before distribution, but never widen access without authorization.

When two sources conflict, show both and name the conflict. For example, a signed service schedule may show one operating window while the current CRM template promises another. The assistant should not select the more convenient record. The owner needs to resolve which commitment governs.

## Make the alternatives comparable

Give each permitted option the same categories. Useful categories may include affected work, timing, cost already approved for discussion, operational dependencies, reversibility, known risks, and evidence gaps. Choose categories that fit the decision rather than forcing every meeting into a standard scorecard.

Avoid converting uncertain judgments into precise scores. A red, amber, or green label helps only when the definitions are written and the underlying evidence remains visible. If two reviewers can apply the same label to different conditions, the color hides disagreement.

Include the status quo as an option when doing nothing has a real consequence. State that consequence in the same terms used for the proposed changes. This prevents the meeting from treating delay as a neutral choice.

## Show what is still missing

An evidence gap is useful when it is specific. "More research needed" does not tell anyone what blocks the decision. Write the missing fact, its owner, the source to check, and the decision effect. If the information cannot arrive before the meeting, ask the owner whether to narrow the decision, defer it, or proceed with the uncertainty recorded.

Imagine that the support-hours pack lacks weekend contact data because an export failed. The assistant can identify the missing period and show which comparison is incomplete. The operations owner decides whether weekday evidence is enough for a limited pilot. The assistant should not fill the gap by extrapolating without an approved method.

## Design the meeting around the choice

Put the decision statement, owner, options, and unresolved questions near the start. Follow with the smallest evidence needed to compare those options. Move detailed history and supporting tables to linked appendices. A participant should be able to understand the decision path without reading every attachment.

Add an agenda segment for questions that must be answered before the choice. Name who will introduce each item. If a specialist owns a legal, financial, security, employment, or compliance judgment, identify that boundary and invite or consult the right person rather than asking the assistant to summarize beyond the source.

Distribute the pack at the approved time and through the approved channel. Confirm recipients against the current attendee list. Late additions require an access check, especially when the pack includes personnel, customer, commercial, or security information.

## Capture the result without rewriting history

During or after the meeting, record the decision, decision-maker, time, conditions, actions, owners, and due dates. Keep the pre-meeting version so later readers can see what evidence was available when the choice was made. Correct factual errors with a visible note instead of silently replacing the original basis.

If the group does not decide, record why. A missing approver, unresolved conflict, or new material fact leads to a different follow-up than simple lack of time. Set the next decision point and identify what must change before then.

Review whether packs arrive on time, links work, sources are current, open questions have owners, and decisions can be traced to the recorded options. Also track material facts discovered during the meeting and decisions reopened because the original pack was incomplete. Page count and attachment count are poor measures of preparation quality.

## Sources and limits

The U.S. Government Accountability Office Green Book discusses quality information, responsibility, control activities, and documentation. NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 addresses governance, information protection, roles, and access. The NIST Privacy Framework addresses privacy-risk management. These sources support traceable information and bounded access. They do not decide a company's supplier, staffing, budget, legal, or customer commitments. Those choices remain with authorized owners and qualified specialists.
