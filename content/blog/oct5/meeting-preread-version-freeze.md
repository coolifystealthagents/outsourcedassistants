# Freeze the right pre-read version before a meeting

A meeting pre-read stops being dependable when contributors keep editing it after distribution begins. Some attendees receive one chart, others see a revised recommendation, and the chair may not know which version is being discussed. A Filipino assistant can manage the release mechanics, but the chair or document owner must decide which substantive changes are approved. A good freeze creates one identifiable reading copy and a controlled path for late evidence.

## Define what the freeze controls

The freeze is not a ban on learning something new. It is a boundary between the material approved for distribution and changes that still need a decision. Write down the meeting, document owner, approver, intended audience, distribution time, source files, and the exact condition that makes a version ready.

That condition may require approved numbers, named recommendations, accessible formatting, working links, and removal of internal comments. A file is not ready merely because somebody moved it into a folder called Final. The assistant should verify the stated checks and collect approvals, not make an editorial judgment when contributors disagree.

Use a stable release identifier that people can read. It might combine the meeting date, a short document name, and a revision number. Record the source location and the released file's hash when the team's tooling supports it. The identifier tells participants which copy to use; the hash helps an operator confirm that two files are byte-for-byte the same. Neither proves the content was approved without the decision record.

## Separate working material from the reading copy

Keep editable source files in their approved workspace. Place the released pre-read in a location where ordinary contributors cannot silently replace it. If the collaboration platform always shows the latest document, distribute a locked export or a clearly marked snapshot according to policy. Do not create a maze of emailed attachments when one controlled link can serve the audience.

The release record should list the source revision, export time, assistant who prepared it, approver, audience, and distribution evidence. Check that links inside the document point to material the audience can access. An external attendee should not receive a link that exposes an internal folder, and an internal attendee should not need a public copy containing fewer controls merely because permissions were tested late.

Accessibility belongs before release. Confirm heading order, descriptive links, table labels, reading order, contrast, and meaningful alternative text under the organization's checklist. A PDF that looks fixed visually may still have broken reading order or inaccessible charts. The assistant can run defined checks and flag failures; a qualified owner decides whether an exception is acceptable.

## Route late changes by consequence

Create three lanes for material arriving after freeze. A correction lane handles an objectively wrong label, broken link, or formatting defect whose approved meaning does not change. A decision lane covers new numbers, altered recommendations, changed risks, or a different requested action. An appendix lane holds useful background that does not belong in the decision document.

Each lane needs an owner. The assistant may prepare a corrected export when the written rule clearly covers the defect, then record the new identifier and redistribute it through the approved method. The assistant should not classify a changed forecast as a typo because the author says it is small. A numerical change can alter the decision even when it affects one cell.

When a late item cannot be approved before the meeting, preserve it separately and tell the chair exactly what is unresolved. Do not blend it into the released copy. The chair can postpone the decision, discuss the new evidence verbally, circulate an addendum, or keep the original scope. Those are meeting decisions, not document housekeeping.

## Work through a changed finance chart

Imagine that the chair approves a pre-read at noon for a 4:00 p.m. review. The assistant exports version `Q4-review-20261005-R1`, checks access, and begins distribution. At 12:20, Finance replaces a revenue chart after finding that one region was counted twice. Two external advisers have already opened R1; internal attendees have not.

The assistant should stop further distribution and record the late change. The report should state which chart changed, why Finance says it changed, who has received R1, and whether the recommendation or requested decision may differ. It should not declare R1 invalid or send R2 immediately. The chair and Finance owner decide whether the meeting can use the corrected chart and whether recipients need an explanation.

If the chair approves a replacement, create R2 from the authorized source, repeat the release checks, and preserve R1 as superseded rather than deleting history. Send a concise correction through the same audience-controlled channel. State that R2 replaces R1, identify the changed section, and ask recipients to use the new copy. Confirm that the controlled link resolves to R2 and that nobody can mistake both versions for current.

Now change the example: Finance altered only the chart's title while all values and meaning remain the same. The written correction lane may permit the document owner to approve that fix without reconvening the chair. The release record still changes. A supposedly minor correction should never make the identity of the distributed copy ambiguous.

## Verify the audience and delivery

Before sending, reconcile the attendee list with the approved audience. Check external guests, assistants attending on behalf of leaders, distribution groups, and people removed from the meeting. Calendar attendance does not automatically grant access to every attachment. Keep confidential annexes on their own permission path when only part of the group may read them.

Distribution evidence should show which release was sent, when, through which channel, and to which approved group. A sent email does not prove that every person opened or understood the document. If the chair requires acknowledgment from key participants, track that separately and escalate gaps before the cutoff.

After distribution, open the recipient-facing copy through an approved test account or permission preview. Confirm the title, release identifier, page completeness, links, images, and accessible text. Do not rely on the editable source looking correct. Export can clip tables, substitute fonts, omit notes, or preserve hidden content.

## Rehearse failure and recovery

Use synthetic meeting packs to test a late number, broken link, inaccessible chart, unauthorized guest, stale attachment, confidential appendix, and correction after partial distribution. Predetermine who approves each response and what recipients see. The assistant's score should reflect accurate release identity, correct audience, complete checks, timely escalation, and clean supersession.

Track releases replaced, late decision changes, correction-only revisions, access failures, recipients who received stale copies, and owner reversals. Review why changes arrived late. The useful fix may be an earlier data cutoff, clearer approval ownership, or a separate appendix—not more reminders from the assistant.

These sources support controlled information, reviewable records, access governance, and accessible digital content. They do not decide whether a late forecast is material or who may approve a business recommendation. The chair, document owner, and organization's meeting policy retain those decisions.
